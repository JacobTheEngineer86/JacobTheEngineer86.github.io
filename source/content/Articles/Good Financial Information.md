# Credit Scores

Your credit score (300-850) represents your credit worthiness and financial reliability based on your borrowing and repayment history. Companies use this score to assess the risk of doing business with you, so it’s one of the most important numbers in your financial life. A good credit score can save you tens of thousands of dollars over your lifetime by qualifying you for lower interest rates on mortgages, auto loans, and credit cards. Your score is calculated based on factors like payment history, credit utilization, length of credit history, types of credit accounts, and recent credit inquiries—and because it affects so many aspects of your financial life, building and maintaining a strong credit score should be a fundamental financial priority.

## FICO and Vantage Credit Scores

There are multiple ways used to calculate a credit score. FICO and Vantage are two main approaches. FICO is the industry standard that most lenders look at. Vantage is less commonly used by lenders (so I just ignore my Vantage score). **Your FICO score should be given more weight than your Vantage score** because lenders generally use the FICO score and not the Vantage score.

To make it even more confusing, there are multiple versions of a FICO score (FICO 8, FICO 9, FICO 10), and a company can choose which FICO score to look at. FICO 8 is currently the most common, but that doesn’t mean all companies use it. 

So, the bottom line is:
1) Work on improving your credit without getting bogged down by the details of which credit score to track, and
2) Trust FICO more than Vantage, and when you need a large loan (like for a house or a car), look deeper into the specific credit score needed for that specific loan. 

Note: I checked my mortgage credit scores online, and they’re about 40 points lower than my Vantage score. So just know that there can be a large difference between the credit scores companies show you and the credit scores that are actually used. 

## The Three Credit Bureaus 

The three credit bureaus - Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion - look at different financial data to calculate your credit score. Whenever a company looks at your credit score, they look at the FICO credit score from these bureaus. 

Note: For big loans, lenders generally look at the middle score of the three bureaus. For example, if you have the following credit scores - Equifax: 740, Experian: 742, TransUnion: 750 - the credit score that lenders use will be 742. 

To see your Experian FICO 8 score, create a free account at https://www.experian.com/. You can also use the Experian app.

To see your Equifax FICO 8 score, create a free account at https://secure.myfico.com/.

Note: You can create a free account at https://www.equifax.com/, but this gives your Vantage score, not your FICO score.

I haven’t found a way yet to see my TransUnion FICO score for free. 

There are paid services to view your FICO score from all three services, like from https://secure.myfico.com/.

Some banks and credit card companies provide a FICO credit score from one of the three bureaus, but it’s not guaranteed. They may provide a Vantage score, which is less useful in my opinion.

Just be wary of credit scores that banks and credit card companies give you. Unless they show the FICO score, I’d just treat it as an estimate. 

## Building Your Credit Score

A high credit score gives you more financial freedom.

You want to aim for a credit score of 740 or above. 

### Key Factors That Affect Your Credit Score

The five key factors that affect your credit score are:

**Payment History (35%)** - Whether you pay your bills on time is the most important factor. Late payments, defaults, and bankruptcies significantly damage your score.

**Credit Utilization (30%)** - This is the ratio of your credit card balances to your credit limits. Keeping this below 30%, and ideally below 10%, helps your score.

**Length of Credit History (15%)** - How long you’ve had credit accounts matters. Older accounts demonstrate more experience managing credit responsibly.

**Credit Mix (10%)** - Having a variety of credit types—credit cards, auto loans, mortgages—shows you can handle different kinds of debt responsibly.

**New Credit Inquiries (10%)** - Applying for multiple credit accounts in a short period can lower your score, as it may signal financial distress or increased risk.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​

The exact percentages may vary, but this gives a good idea. 

### Hard and Soft Inquiries

There are two types of credit checks:
- Soft inquiries - Checking your own credit, pre-approved credit offers, or employment background checks. These don’t affect your score.
- Hard inquiries - When you apply for credit (loans, credit cards, mortgages), lenders check your credit, and these can temporarily lower your score by a few points.

### Tips for Building Credit with Credit Cards
- If you don’t already have a credit card, GET ONE. One of the biggest factors that impacts your credit score is the length of time you’ve been building credit.
	- Getting a credit card generally requires a hard inquiry, which temporarily lowers your credit score. 
- Never miss a payment. This is the biggest thing. Setup automatic payments.
- Keep your credit utilization at least under 30%, and ideally under 10%. 
- Stay on top of requesting credit limit increases.
	- Credit card companies have different ways of increasing your credit limit. Find what your credit card company’s process is for increasing your credit limit, and make sure to request increases as often as you can. 

- Opening too many credit cards at once can hurt your credit score because:
	1.	Multiple hard inquiries - Each application triggers a hard inquiry, which temporarily lowers your score by a few points
	2.	Reduced average age of accounts - New accounts lower your average account age, which impacts the “Length of Credit History” factor
	3.	Perceived risk - Lenders may view someone opening many accounts quickly as a risk signal (potential financial distress or intention to rack up debt)

#### Advanced Tips for Building Credit with Credit Cards
The key takeaway is to never miss a credit card payment and to keep your credit utilization below 30%. Focus on that. But if you want to maximize your credit score, here is the optimal payment strategy.

1.	Use your credit cards regularly for everyday purchases to show active credit use
2.	Keep balances low - ideally under 10% of your credit limit, and definitely under 30%. This is what matters for your utilization ratio when your statement generates.
3.	Let the statement generate - don’t pay everything off before the statement closes, or it may report as $0 balance (no activity)
4.	Pay the full statement balance before the due date - this avoids interest charges while demonstrating responsible payment behavior
5.	Never miss a payment - set up autopay for at least the minimum to protect your payment history, which is the most important factor

Optional optimization: If your balance is high before the statement closes, you can make a payment to lower it before the statement generates (reducing reported utilization), then pay the remaining statement balance in full by the due date.

## Credit Reports

Your credit reports do NOT give you your FICO credit scores. 

Checking your own credit reports is encouraged as a smart financial habit. You’re entitled to one free report from each of the three major credit bureaus (Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion) every year through annualcreditreport.com, and reviewing them regularly helps you catch errors, monitor for identity theft, and understand what’s affecting your score—all without any negative impact.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​
